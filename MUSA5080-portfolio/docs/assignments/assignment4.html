ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}

The outcome I am interpreting is the percentage of households experiencing rent burden which can be defined as 30% or more of their income goes to rent. A number of different agencies look into rent burden. Federal Agencies like Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia that specifically has a Rental Housing Affordability data tool and US HUD which uses rent burden as a metric if where the country is at economically. Local organizations like Office of Homeless Services and Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation would use this to identify areas and populations that may benefit from emergency or monthly rent subsidies. The outcome is moderately reliable with 327 tracts in moderate reliability, 22 in high reliability, and 59 in low reliability.

There is clustering of high negative residuals in the City Center and Old City meaning that the model is over predicting. This means that the rent burden is actually lower than what the model predicts. This result may be due to higher income renters living in Center City. In other blue areas, it could be possible that public housing programs are implemented in which subsided rent can stay low, meaning burden is also low.

Additionally there is red clustering in Far Northeast Philadelphia and parts of West Philadelphia, meaning there is high positive residuals and the model is under predicting in these areas. This result is likely due to variables not included in my model, like students paying market rent with little to no income, new development in neighborhoods like Fishtown which may out pace incomes.

These two layers that I do added that where not covered in my ACS predictors are rental building licenses and food retail. The most direct variable from Carto is rental licenses as in building permits to build new rental apartments and developments. Part 7: Reflection

The feature that lowered the CV RMSE the most was the log of rental license density, bringing it from 16.37 at baseline to 16.20. Without the log, rental density made the model slightly worse at 16.38. The raw density was right-skewed, and logging it let the model use differences between typical tracts instead of being pulled by a few extreme ones. This makes sense to me, since heavy rental licensing shows where landlords and new rental development are concentrated, which can push rents up faster than incomes. I was disappointed but not surprised that distance to high-produce grocery stores added noise, raising the CV RMSE to 16.40. Food access says more about neighborhood amenities than about rent and income. There could have been better features to use, but I was inspired by the data available on OpenDataPhilly. In my final model, the coefficient on single-parent households is 0.54. Holding the other variables constant, each additional percentage point of single-parent households is associated with a 0.54 percentage point higher share of renters spending 30% or more of their income on rent. For two otherwise identical tracts, the model predicts the one with 10 more percentage points of single-parent households to have a rent burden rate about 5.4 points higher. My residual maps showed clustering near universities, but I chose to investigate other variables first. In the future, I would add distance to the nearest university using OpenDataPhilly’s university locations, since students often report little income while paying market rent. Lastly, the fog plot shows that most tracts have a low CV, meaning their census estimates are reliable, yet the model still misses many of them. So most of the remaining errors likely come from things the model does not measure. The two tracts with CVs near 300 probably have very few renters, so their large misses reflect unreliable census estimates more than the model.

#| label: cv-helper
ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}

## Part 7: Reflection

The feature that lowered the CV RMSE the most was the log of rental license density, bringing it from 16.37 at baseline to 16.20. Without the log, rental density made the model slightly worse at 16.38. The raw density was right-skewed, and logging it let the model use differences between typical tracts instead of being pulled by a few extreme ones. This makes sense to me, since heavy rental licensing shows where landlords and new rental development are concentrated, which can push rents up faster than incomes. I was disappointed but not surprised that distance to high-produce grocery stores added noise, raising the CV RMSE to 16.40. Food access says more about neighborhood amenities than about rent and income. There could have been better features to use, but I was inspired by the data available on OpenDataPhilly. In my final model, the coefficient on single-parent households is 0.54. Holding the other variables constant, each additional percentage point of single-parent households is associated with a 0.54 percentage point higher share of renters spending 30% or more of their income on rent. For two otherwise identical tracts, the model predicts the one with 10 more percentage points of single-parent households to have a rent burden rate about 5.4 points higher. My residual maps showed clustering near universities, but I chose to investigate other variables first. In the future, I would add distance to the nearest university using OpenDataPhilly’s university locations, since students often report little income while paying market rent. Lastly, the fog plot shows that most tracts have a low CV, meaning their census estimates are reliable, yet the model still misses many of them. So most of the remaining errors likely come from things the model does not measure. The two tracts with CVs near 300 probably have very few renters, so their large misses reflect unreliable census estimates more than the model.
